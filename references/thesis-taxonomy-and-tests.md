# Thesis taxonomy and tests

Use this reference when generating or comparing thesis candidates. It prevents a broad “good thesis” label from hiding different standards across academic, research, and opinion genres.

## Core distinctions

| Item | What it answers | Example form | Not enough by itself |
|---|---|---|---|
| Topic | What area is under discussion? | Assessment dashboards | It does not say what the piece claims |
| Research question | What does the inquiry seek to know? | How do dashboards change decision behavior? | It may remain unanswered |
| Observation | What appears to be happening? | Schools use dashboards unevenly | It may be descriptive rather than argumentative |
| Thesis/claim | What should the reader accept? | Dashboards improve decisions only when uncertainty is visible | It still needs evidence and warrant |
| Warrant | Why does the evidence support the claim? | Decision quality depends on how users interpret signal and uncertainty | It is not a source unless separately supported |
| Hypothesis | What testable relationship is predicted? | Visible uncertainty will reduce overconfident decisions | It is a prediction, not a finding |
| Implication | Why does the claim matter? | Reporting uncertainty changes implementation priorities | It must follow from the claim |
| Recommendation/ask | What should happen? | Require uncertainty displays before high-stakes use | It needs feasibility, authority, and value reasoning |

The example in this table is **HYPOTHETICAL** and is included only to show form. Replace it with project-specific evidence.

## Candidate thesis types

### Academic interpretive or analytical thesis

Claims what a text, case, dataset, concept, or practice means, reveals, complicates, or makes visible. It should identify the object and interpretive move, not simply summarize. Evidence is usually close reading, comparison, documented observation, or a bounded corpus.

Diagnostic questions:

- What is the object of interpretation?
- What pattern or tension is easy to miss?
- What alternative reading is plausible?
- What specific evidence makes this interpretation defensible?

### Academic explanatory or causal thesis

Claims why or under what conditions something happens. It must respect design strength: observation, association, mechanism, quasi-experiment, experiment, and theory do not support identical causal language.

Diagnostic questions:

- What is the outcome and what is the proposed cause or mechanism?
- Is the evidence temporal, comparative, experimental, qualitative, or correlational?
- What confounders, boundary conditions, and counterexamples matter?
- Should the verb be `causes`, `contributes to`, `is consistent with`, `helps explain`, or `may reflect`?

### Comparative or evaluative thesis

Judges two or more cases, methods, policies, texts, or options against explicit criteria. The criteria and comparison class must be visible; otherwise the conclusion becomes preference disguised as analysis.

Diagnostic questions:

- Compared with what, for whom, and under which conditions?
- What criteria are being applied and why are they legitimate?
- Which trade-offs prevent a simple winner?

### Methodological or measurement thesis

Claims what a measure, model, method, construct, or design does or does not warrant. Separate construct, operationalization, estimate, validity evidence, decision rule, and consequence.

Diagnostic questions:

- What is the construct and what is actually observed?
- What validity evidence supports the intended interpretation and use?
- What uncertainty, subgroup pattern, threshold, or downstream consequence limits the claim?
- Is the unit of evidence a case, sample, population, or decision context?

### Normative, policy, or design thesis

Claims what ought to be valued, chosen, changed, or built. It requires both factual premises and a value/warrant bridge. Do not hide a normative choice inside a supposedly neutral empirical claim.

Diagnostic questions:

- What value, right, goal, or constraint makes the recommendation desirable?
- Who bears the cost, risk, and implementation burden?
- What feasible alternatives were considered?
- What would count as evidence that the proposal worked or caused harm?

### Opinion thesis

Combines a public diagnosis or interpretation with a reason to care now and a feasible implication. It should be specific enough to be a proposition, public enough to matter to the target audience, and bounded enough to defend in the chosen form.

Diagnostic questions:

- What do I want this audience to believe, reconsider, or do?
- Why now, and what is the credible peg?
- What is new or distinctive about this argument?
- What evidence and experience authorize the writer to make it?
- What is the strongest fair objection, and what is the answer or concession?

## Hard-gate tests

### Claim identity test

Fill in: “This piece is asking the reader to accept that ___.” If the blank produces a topic, description, slogan, truism, or unsupported feeling, the candidate is not ready.

### Arguability test

Name a thoughtful person who could disagree and state the disagreement accurately. “Someone could disagree because they are ignorant” is not a counterargument. A claim can be contestable without being artificially polarizing.

### Scope test

Underline every noun and verb that carries scope. Specify:

- who/what is included;
- where and when;
- which construct or outcome;
- which comparison or baseline;
- whether the claim is case-specific or general;
- whether the evidence supports description, association, mechanism, causation, value, or recommendation.

Replace universal language (`always`, `everyone`, `proves`, `the answer`) unless the evidence truly warrants it.

### Warrant test

Write the bridge as `Because [evidence or premise], therefore [claim]`. Then ask:

- Is the “because” actually supported?
- Is a value judgment being presented as a fact?
- Is a case being treated as a population?
- Is an association being treated as a cause?
- Does the audience need the warrant stated explicitly?

### Evidence-fit test

Map the claim to the minimum evidence it requires:

| Claim | Minimum fit question |
|---|---|
| Descriptive | Does the source observe or measure the thing in the stated setting and period? |
| Interpretive | Does the corpus contain enough detail to support the reading and a rival reading? |
| Comparative | Are cases and criteria commensurable, and are differences meaningful? |
| Associational | Is the relationship measured with appropriate variables and uncertainty? |
| Causal | Is there a credible design or mechanism, and are alternative explanations addressed? |
| Normative | Are factual premises, values, trade-offs, and affected parties visible? |
| Recommendation | Is there authority, feasibility, risk control, and a way to evaluate consequences? |

Mark each source as `DIRECT`, `PARAPHRASE`, `INFERRED`, `HYPOTHETICAL`, or `NEEDS-SOURCE`. A source that only supports a weaker claim cannot be counted as full support for a stronger one.

### Defensibility test

State the strongest objection in the opponent’s vocabulary, then answer it without caricature. The response may be rebuttal, qualification, concession, reframing, or a decision to narrow. If the thesis depends on ignoring the objection, it fails.

### Originality test

Compare the candidate with the screened corpus on four dimensions:

1. **Object:** a new case, population, text, construct, or decision context.
2. **Relation:** a new comparison, mechanism, causal explanation, or tension.
3. **Interpretation:** a new reading or synthesis of known material.
4. **Use:** a new implication, design, policy, or audience-specific application.

Record what is genuinely new and what is inherited. If the search is incomplete, use `ORIGINALITY: UNVERIFIED`; do not convert a search gap into novelty.

### Audience-usefulness test

Complete: “For this audience, this claim changes ___.” The answer can be understanding, interpretation, choice, practice, or attention. It cannot be only “it is interesting.” If no useful change exists, reconsider the audience, purpose, or thesis.

### Edge test

Ask what the candidate refuses to claim. A thesis with no edge is often a topic or slogan. The edge can be a boundary, condition, trade-off, exception, or rival explanation.

## Comparative scoring after gates

Score only candidates that pass the hard gates. Use a 1–5 score and a one-sentence reason for every score:

| Dimension | 1 | 3 | 5 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Scope fit | Unbounded or mismatched | Bounded with repair | Exact fit to task and evidence |
| Original contribution | Repeats known point | Useful synthesis or application | Clear, verified contribution relative to corpus |
| Defensibility | Strong objection defeats it | Objection can be qualified | Strongest objection improves the claim |
| Evidence fit | Evidence is absent or wrong type | Adequate but thin or mixed | Multiple fit-for-purpose supports with limits |
| Explanatory power | Labels rather than explains | Identifies a plausible relationship | Makes mechanism, stakes, and conditions visible |
| Audience utility | No clear consequence | Relevant but diffuse | Changes a decision, understanding, or action |
| Narrative potential | No meaningful movement | One usable tension or sequence | Thesis naturally creates question, turn, and payoff |

Do not sum scores into a mechanical winner. Use the matrix to compare trade-offs. A hard-gate failure always overrides a high score.

## Candidate comparison questions

Before choosing, answer:

1. Which candidate makes the most important claim the evidence can actually carry?
2. Which candidate teaches the audience something they did not already know?
3. Which candidate has the clearest and most defensible warrant?
4. Which candidate has the strongest fair counterargument—and can use it productively?
5. Which candidate is narrow enough to finish in the actual medium and length?
6. Which candidate supports a hook without sensationalism?
7. Which candidate gives Narrative Engine a real movement rather than a decorative arc?
8. What would have to be researched before adopting the apparent winner?

## Red flags

- “This paper will discuss…” with no assertion.
- A thesis that is only a fact nobody disputes.
- A value preference presented as a universal empirical finding.
- A claim that needs a book-length literature review in a short piece.
- `Proves`, `solves`, `the best`, or `the only` when evidence is limited.
- A vivid anecdote carrying a population-level conclusion.
- A recommendation without a stated value, authority, feasibility, or risk.
- A title that makes a stronger claim than the thesis.
- A conclusion that introduces a new thesis or population.
- A candidate whose originality exists only because the search was not done.
