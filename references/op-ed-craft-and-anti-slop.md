# Op-ed craft and anti-slop

The goal is not decorative originality. It is a precise argument whose language feels observed, chosen, and alive. Use this reference during architecture and revision.

## Craft principles

- Lead with the most consequential question or concrete pressure, not a ceremonial throat-clearing paragraph.
- Let the thesis become findable early; do not force the reader to infer the point from atmosphere.
- Give paragraphs jobs: establish, complicate, evidence, compare, concede, reframe, apply, or close.
- Make actors visible. Prefer “the department changed the rule” to abstractions that conceal agency.
- Prefer exact nouns and active verbs, but keep technical terms when they carry necessary meaning.
- Vary sentence length and paragraph shape in response to thought, not random ornament.
- Use a memorable phrase only when it compresses a real insight or makes a consequential distinction.
- Let humor, anger, tenderness, irony, or awe arise from the material and the writer’s relation to it.
- Use counterargument to improve the claim. Concede what is true, narrow what is too broad, and rebut what the evidence actually answers.
- End on an earned consequence, image, or decision—not a generic call to action.

## Anti-slop audit

The draft fails until repaired if it contains any of the following as unexamined defaults:

- canned openings (“in today’s rapidly changing world,” “we stand at a crossroads,” or equivalent);
- generic transitions that announce structure instead of making thought move;
- abstract praise (“important,” “innovative,” “transformative,” “urgent”) without a concrete referent;
- false balance or reflexive hedging used to avoid making a claim;
- default three-part lists, mirrored sentences, or symmetrical paragraphs that flatten the material;
- corporate uplift, consultant fog, institutional passive voice, or AI-sounding filler;
- a parade of rhetorical questions with no real answer;
- em-dash, semicolon, colon, or exclamation habits that become a signature by accident;
- a personal voice unsupported by actual relationship, observation, or disclosure;
- invented anecdotes, composite characters presented as real, unsupported statistics, fabricated quotes, or “human” detail not in the source record;
- metaphors that could belong to any op-ed on any subject;
- a conclusion that escalates beyond the evidence because the draft wants a larger ending.

Do not respond to anti-slop checks by replacing every sentence with the same short, blunt rhythm. Human writing includes variation, qualification, technical density, lyric moments, and occasional awkwardness with purpose.

## Revision passes

1. **Claim pass:** What is this piece built to prove? What does each section do for that claim?
2. **Evidence pass:** Are facts, numbers, quotations, dates, and attributions checked and proportionate? Is uncertainty preserved?
3. **Reader pass:** What question is the reader carrying at each turn? Where does attention drop because the next move is predictable or unearned?
4. **Voice pass:** Which sentences could only have come from this writer and this corpus? Protect them; make the rest more specific.
5. **Line pass:** Remove dead words, bureaucratic fog, hidden actors, repeated qualifiers, and decorative abstraction. Read aloud.
6. **Slop pass:** Search for canned phrases, default symmetry, generic transitions, reflex hedges, inflated stakes, and pasted-in language. Then read the whole piece again for natural movement.

## Final question

Would an informed reader recognize a real mind, a real stake, and a real body of evidence here—or only a competent imitation of an opinion piece?
