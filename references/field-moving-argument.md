# Field-moving argument

A field-moving opinion thesis does not need to be revolutionary. It must make a consequential difference to an informed reader’s model of the issue. “I have not seen this sentence before” is not enough.

## Angle lattice

Map the conversation before selecting the angle:

| Dimension | Question | Evidence to record |
|---|---|---|
| Current frame | What explanation, diagnosis, or debate currently organizes attention? | Key sources, repeated terms, dominant assumptions |
| Overlooked fact | What important fact, population, cost, mechanism, or contradiction is underweighted? | Source IDs and limitation |
| Reconnection | What two bodies of evidence are rarely read together? | Shared construct, tension, or bridge |
| Mechanism | What explains the pattern better than the default account? | Warrant and evidence fit |
| Consequence | What changes for a person, institution, policy, or decision? | Bounded implication |
| Authority | Why can this writer see or explain this connection? | Experience, expertise, access, or transparent limitation |
| Ask | What should a reader understand, test, reconsider, or do? | Feasible action, not a slogan |

A candidate becomes stronger when it changes at least one of the field’s frame, mechanism, comparison, consequence, or decision. It becomes weaker when it only intensifies language around the existing frame.

## Five originality tests

1. **Replaceability:** could another competent writer produce the same claim from the same obvious sources? If yes, find the missing connection or narrow the claim.
2. **Counter-frame:** can the strongest existing explanation be stated fairly? If not, the candidate may be attacking a straw man.
3. **New consequence:** does the candidate produce a different practical, ethical, or intellectual implication?
4. **Evidence delta:** which source, observation, comparison, or synthesis makes this candidate possible?
5. **Reader update:** write the before/after belief. If the after statement is only “this matters a lot,” the contribution is not yet specific.

## Argument choreography

For a public argument, plan the pressure sequence rather than a generic five-paragraph essay:

1. **Promise:** give the reader a concrete problem, contradiction, or stake.
2. **Orientation:** establish enough context to prevent a false inference.
3. **Thesis:** state the proposition early enough to guide reading.
4. **Evidence ladder:** move from concrete detail to pattern, mechanism, or comparison; name scope and source.
5. **Pressure:** introduce the strongest objection where it tests the thesis, not where a template demands it.
6. **Turn/reframe:** show what the objection changes, reveals, or cannot explain.
7. **Ask:** translate the argument into a bounded implication or action.
8. **Close:** return to the opening object/question with changed meaning, or end on the clearest earned consequence.

The sequence may be rearranged when the material demands it. The rule is functional movement, not a fixed order.

## Candidate matrix additions

Add these columns to the normal thesis matrix:

- current frame;
- contribution type;
- evidence delta;
- reader update;
- strongest fair counter-frame;
- authority/relationship;
- likely outlet fit;
- originality status: `VERIFIED`, `PLAUSIBLE`, or `UNVERIFIED`.

## What counts as movement

A thesis can move a field by:

- exposing a mechanism beneath a familiar outcome;
- joining evidence that is usually kept in separate conversations;
- changing the unit of attention from institution to person, or person to system, without erasing either;
- identifying a neglected trade-off or distributional consequence;
- translating a technical result into a decision that has been framed poorly;
- correcting an overconfident public story while preserving the valid concern beneath it;
- making a feasible recommendation that follows from evidence rather than from preference; or
- giving a lived experience a precise explanatory place without using it as ornamental proof.

## Stop conditions

Do not claim a field-moving contribution when the search is incomplete, the key comparator is missing, the contribution depends on an unverified fact, or the proposed “newness” is only a novel metaphor. Label the gap and choose `DEFER`, `NARROW`, or `REVISE`.
